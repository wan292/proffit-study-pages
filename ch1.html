The Changing Goals of Orthodontic Treatment

I The Development of Orthodontics

  • Edward H. Angle — "father of modern orthodontics." His classification of malocclusion (1890s) was pivotal: it subdivided major malocclusion types and gave the first clear, simple definition of normal occlusion — "the mesiobuccal cusp of the upper first molar occludes in the buccal groove of the lower molar, and the teeth lie on a smoothly curving line of occlusion."
  • Normal occlusion and Class I malocclusion share the same molar relationship but differ in tooth arrangement relative to the line of occlusion Fig 1.3. The line of occlusion may or may not be correct in Class II / Class III.
  • Angle and his followers strongly opposed extraction, and facial proportions/esthetics received less attention. Charles Tweed and Raymond Begg later reintroduced extraction to improve facial esthetics and occlusal stability.
Expanded history (7th) The 7th edition broadens the early history: Kingsley and Calvin Case stressed facial esthetics and judicious extraction for dentofacial harmony, and Paul Simon tied the dentition to the skull — early recognition that the teeth must relate to the face (and the first lessons about retention/relapse).

II Modern Treatment Goals: The Soft-Tissue Paradigm

  • The soft-tissue paradigm: the goals and limitations of modern orthodontic/orthognathic treatment are set by the soft tissues of the face — not by the teeth and bones Table 1.1. Its effects on planning:
    1. The primary goal becomes soft-tissue relationships and adaptations — not Angle's ideal occlusion.
    2. The secondary goal becomes functional occlusion.
    3. The thought process: establish the desired soft-tissue relationships first, then determine how teeth and jaws must be arranged to meet them — which maps directly onto why patients and parents seek treatment.
Epidemiology of Malocclusion
  • NHANES III characterized malocclusion using the irregularity index (a measure of incisor alignment) Fig 1.4:
    • Only 34% of adults have well-aligned lower incisors; nearly 15% of adolescents/adults have severely or extremely irregular incisors.
    • A midline diastema is common in childhood (26% have a >2 mm space).
    • At most 30% have Angle's normal occlusion. Class I (50–55%) is by far the largest group; Class II (~15%) is about half as common as normal occlusion; Class III (<1%) is a very small proportion.
    • Class II is most prevalent in whites of northern European descent; Class III in Asian populations; Class III and open bite are more frequent in those of African descent.
Who Needs Treatment?

I Psychological Problems

  • Severe malocclusion is likely to be a social handicap. The major reason people seek orthodontic treatment is to minimize psychosocial problems related to dental/facial appearance — these are not "just cosmetic" and can have a major effect on quality of life.

II Oral Function

  • Severe malocclusion affects oral function, but function adapts to form surprisingly well (mastication, speech, lip–tongue posture) — malocclusion usually makes function difficult, not impossible, requiring extra effort to compensate.
  • For the great majority, there is no association between malocclusion and TMD. Orthodontics as the primary treatment for TMD is almost never indicated — though in special circumstances it can be a useful adjunct to other treatment for muscle pain.

III Relationship to Dental Injury & Disease

  • About a 1-in-3 chance that a child with untreated Class II will suffer upper-incisor trauma. With previous trauma and age <9 yr, the risk of additional trauma is 8.4× higher — for such a child, retracting the incisors (not growth modification) is indicated.
  • Malocclusion has little if any impact on caries or supporting structures, and only a tenuous link to later major periodontal disease. Long-term studies show orthodontic treatment does not increase the chance of later periodontal problems.
  • Summary: psychosocial and functional handicaps can create significant need for treatment; the evidence that orthodontics reduces later dental disease is less clear.
Type of Treatment: Evidence-Based Selection
  • A hierarchy of evidence quality guides clinical decisions Fig 1.19: the unsupported opinion of an expert is the weakest form; the randomized clinical trial (patients randomly assigned in advance to alternatives) is the gold standard — with systematic reviews/meta-analyses of RCTs at the top.

Demand for Treatment
  • Two major methods now score malocclusion severity: the PAR (Peer Assessment Rating, UK) and the ABO discrepancy index (US). Both were developed to objectively measure improvement during treatment but correlate reasonably with expert opinion of treatment need. Both consider just the dentition — not skeletal or facial characteristics.
  • The Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN) (Brook & Shaw, UK) was designed to evaluate need for treatment, with two parts:
    • a dental health component — grades 1 (no need) → 5 (extreme/need treatment) Box 1.1;
    • an esthetic component — the standard attractiveness photographs Fig 1.25.
