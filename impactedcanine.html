The Impacted Maxillary Canine

The Ericson & Kurol canon in 5 minutes · prevalence → diagnosis → prognosis

The 60-second version

1.7%of children have an ectopic / impacted maxillary canine
2:1female : male
~85%of these canines are palatal, not buccal
12% → 38%lateral-incisor resorption: plain film vs CT
78%normalise after extracting the primary canine
91% / 64%success if canine overlaps <½ vs >½ of the lateral root
  • The one big idea. Ericson & Kurol's lasting contribution is that the damage an ectopic canine does to the neighbouring incisor roots is massively under-diagnosed on conventional radiographs. Move from plain films to CT and the resorption rate on lateral incisors jumps from ~12% to 38% — and most of what you were missing is severe. E&K 1987 · 2000
  • The clinical trigger. At about age 10, a maxillary primary canine that is not mobile with no palpable facial bulge = suspect ectopic eruption. That is the whole screening test. Proffit Ch11
  • The intervention. Extract the primary canine — and the earlier and less mesially displaced the permanent canine, the better it works. Proffit Ch11
"Palpate at 10, extract the C, and trust CT over the panoramic."

Which papers, actually?

"The Ericson & Kurol article" is really a run of four landmark papers from the same two Swedish authors. Proffit cites them without giving the year, so know what each one contributed.

  • 1986 — Longitudinal study and analysis of clinical supervision of maxillary canine eruption. Community Dent Oral Epidemiol
    → gave the prevalence and established palpation from age 10–11 as the screening method.
  • 1987 — Radiographic examination of ectopically erupting maxillary canines. Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop
    → gave the sector and alpha-angle measurements on the panoramic; found resorption in ~12% of laterals.
  • 1988 — Early treatment of palatally erupting maxillary canines by extraction of the primary canines. Eur J Orthod
    → the famous 78% interceptive-extraction paper. This is the one usually meant by "the Ericson & Kurol article."
  • 2000 — Resorption of incisors after ectopic eruption of maxillary canines: a CT study. Angle Orthod
    → the under-diagnosis paper: CT reveals far more resorption than plain films.

  • The maxillary canine is the second most commonly impacted tooth, after the third molar. lit
  • Sex ratio ≈ 2:1 female — falls straight out of the 1.17% vs 0.51% split. E&K 1986
  • The palatal-vs-buccal split varies a lot between populations (series report anywhere from ~60% to ~85% palatal). Proffit gives no percentage — if an exam demands one, quote the range, not a single number. lit
  • Associated findings — small or missing lateral incisors, and dental anomalies cluster because they are genetically related (Proffit's stated reason for taking a full panoramic first). Proffit Ch11
  • Laterality clue: palatal — but not buccal — impaction is more prevalent on the side with delayed dental development. Earlier eruption of the opposite canine shoves the incisors toward the delayed side and steals its space. Proffit Ch3
"1.7% overall · girls twice as often · mostly palatal." Compare with Proffit's own rounding: 1–2%.

2 · Diagnosis

The clinical screen — this is the examinable bit

  • At approximately age 10: if the maxillary primary canine is not mobile and there is no observable or palpable facial canine bulge → consider ectopic eruption. Proffit Ch11
  • Two things can then go wrong, and they are not the same problem: (1) impaction of the canine, (2) resorption of the permanent lateral and/or central incisor roots. Proffit Ch11

The imaging ladder

  1. Panoramic first — get the whole picture, because dental anomalies are genetically related and travel together. Proffit Ch11
  2. Then small field-of-view CBCT, for root resorption and true 3-D canine position. Proffit Ch11 (7th ed)
  3. Parallax is the 2-D alternative — horizontal tube shift (two periapicals, SLOB rule) or the right-angle technique (periapical + occlusal). See the radiology lecture page. lit

The Ericson & Kurol panoramic measurements

Three variables off a single panoramic — this is what made the 1987 paper stick.

Sector = where the canine cusp tip sits mesiodistally, read against the long axis of the erupted lateral incisor. Sectors 1–2 lie distal to that axis; 3–5 progressively mesial. Higher sector = worse prognosis.
  • Sector (1–5) — mesiodistal position of the canine crown relative to the lateral incisor's long axis. E&K 1987
  • Alpha angle (α) — angulation of the canine's long axis to the midline. Roughly: <20–30° favourable, >30° unfavourable. E&K 1987 threshold varies by study
  • Vertical height — distance of the cusp tip from the occlusal plane. Higher = worse. E&K 1987
Three axes: how mesial (sector) · how tipped (α) · how high (vertical). All three bad = don't bother intercepting, go straight to expose-and-traction.

3 · The under-diagnosis story — resorption of the incisors

This is the finding the article is famous for. The 2000 CT study looked at 107 children (9–15 y, mean 12.5) with 156 ectopically erupting canines.

  • CT detects roughly 50% more resorption than intraoral films. That gap is the paper's message. E&K 2000
  • And what you miss is the bad stuff. Of the resorbed lateral incisors: 31% slight · 9% moderate · 60% severe, with pulpal involvement. E&K 2000
  • Most resorption sits in the middle third of the root (~82%), not the apex — which is exactly why a 2-D film, superimposing the canine crown over that zone, hides it. E&K 1987
  • Lateral-incisor resorption is about 3× more common in girls. E&K 1987
  • Proffit's consequence: if resorption is already happening, interceptive extraction is no longer the answer — surgically expose the canine and apply orthodontic traction. Proffit Ch11, Fig 11.44 / 11.47
"12 on film, 38 on CT — and 60% of what you find is severe." The panoramic is a screening tool, not a resorption tool.

4 · Prognosis — does extracting the primary canine work?

The 1988 paper: 46 ectopic canines in 35 children, aged 10.0–13.0 (mean 11.4), all with no or minor space loss, reviewed 6-monthly.

78%normalised overall (36 of 46)
91%if overlap < ½ the lateral root
64%if overlap > ½ the lateral root
  • The Proffit-stated version — memorise this one. If the permanent canine crown overlaps less than half of the lateral incisor root on the panoramic, there is an excellent chance — 91% — that the eruption path normalises after extracting the primary canine. If more than half the lateral root is overlapped, that falls to 64%. Proffit Ch11
  • The paper's own headline: 78% (36/46) of palatally erupting canines converted to a normal path after extraction of the primary canine — 23 improved by 6 months, another 13 by 12 months. E&K 1988
  • If nothing has changed at 12 months, it isn't going to. That is the practical review interval. E&K 1988
  • A more advanced sector or a more mesially inclined canine reduces the chance of eruption after extraction — prognosis is a gradient, not a yes/no. E&K 1987/88
  • Proffit's separate, softer claim: early extraction of primary canines decreases the chance of canine impaction, but you need a lingual arch to hold the space and keep the non-extraction option open. Proffit Ch12
"Half a lateral root is the line. Less than half → 91%. More than half → 64%. Overall → 78%."

5 · When interception fails — expose and pull

  • Space first. With a canine already impacted and the primary C recently lost, the first step is to provide / open / maintain the space — before any heavy wire, before any traction. Proffit Ch15
  • Then expose. Ideally the fixed appliance is already in place before exposure so force can be applied immediately; otherwise start active movement no later than 2–3 weeks post-op. Proffit Ch15
  • Labial canine → apically repositioned flap so attached gingiva is carried to the crown; a very high labial canine can use the tunnel method. Needs a heavy stabilising archwire (≥18-mil round steel, preferably rectangular) at operation. Proffit Ch15
  • Palatal canine → open exposure is fine (heavy palatal mucosa isn't a problem), and because open exposure often produces downward drift, immediate active traction can be deferred in many of these. Proffit Ch15
  • Incomplete root? Delaying traction to a palatally impacted canine with an incomplete root is recommended — but favourable spontaneous movement rarely occurs once root formation is complete. Proffit Ch15
  • Attachment: a button or eyelet (7th ed wording), not a bracket, and never a wire ligature round the crown — that costs periodontal attachment and invites ankylosis. Proffit Ch15
  • Mechanics: auxiliary NiTi overlaid on a stabilising base arch is the most efficient method. Proffit Ch15
  • Sequence to recite: open the space → expose → bond a button → traction with an auxiliary NiTi over a heavy base wire, starting immediately (or ≤2–3 weeks).

⌕ How strong is this evidence, really?

  • The 1988 interceptive-extraction study was a case series of 35 children with no control group. Some palatal canines correct on their own, so the untreated comparison is exactly what's missing.
  • Later systematic reviews do find a higher proportion of successful eruption after primary-canine extraction, but with wide confidence intervals — the effect is real-looking, not nailed down.
  • The 1.7% prevalence is from a Swedish population. Palatal-vs-buccal proportions in particular do not transfer cleanly to other populations.
  • For an exam, quote the numbers as above. For a viva, knowing why the 1988 design is weak is the mark that separates a pass from a good pass.

⚠ Confusable traps — don't swap these

  • 91% / 64% (Proffit's overlap-of-the-lateral-root criterion) vs 78% (the paper's overall success). Same study family, three different denominators — don't merge them.
  • 12% vs 38% resorption — 12% is the plain-film figure (1987), 38% is the CT figure (2000). Quoting 12% as "the" resorption rate is the classic error.
  • 38% laterals vs 9% centrals — and 93% contact with the lateral root. Contact is not resorption; nearly all of them touch, only about a third resorb.
  • Age 10 = palpation. Not age 8, not age 12. The trigger is immobile primary canine + no palpable bulge.
  • Sector 1 is the good end. Higher sector number = more mesial = worse. Reversing the scale is an easy mark to lose.
  • Delay traction for a palatal canine with an incomplete root — but don't wait once the root is complete. Proffit's wording is a conditional, not a blanket rule.
  • Canine impaction 1–2% vs supernumerary 90% in the anterior maxilla — two different maxillary anterior facts, frequently conflated in the recall bank.
  • In an OPG showing both a mesiodens and impacted canines, past papers key the mesiodens as the most clinically significant finding — but in the case with retained primary canines + supernumerary, the impacted canines are the key feature. Read which case you're in. recall bank
